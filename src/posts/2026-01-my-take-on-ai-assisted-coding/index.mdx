Just like everyone else in the observable universe, I've been increasingly seeing, reading about, and using AI tools. It seems they're seeping through every aspect our lives, like digital microplastics.

I'm not an LLM expert, and as such this is not in any way a "here's my prediction for this year" kind of post. Hopefully it's the opposite of that.

# All the hyperbole...

Let me just get this part out of the way quickly: I can't stand the average marketing pitch of whatever the latest AI novelty is.
I couldn't care less for the typical social media post by a CEO who spent a weekend vibe-coding and is suddendly an expert in 3 new fields.

I'm exhausted that every conversation somehow shifts into AI.

And no, [I don't want my coffee machine to talk to me in the morning][coffee-ai]. I like my coffee as dumb as a rock, thank you very much.

At the same time, I am intensivelly using, and pushing others to use coding assistants. I will probably continue to do so for the foreseeable future. This is the dichotomy that I haven't felt in previous hype cycles.

Despite me and many others finding this so incredibly useful, the industry still feels pressured to tell us this is much more amazing than what we already see for ourselves.
It's not enough that we got a truly revoluionary tool in so many different ways. We need to convince everyone that utopia is right around the corner, all jobs are deprecated, and money won't be a thing anymore.

One area where I've seen a lot of variance is in how much success different developers have had with AI tooling. One person will claim great success in complex high-value tasks, while another will just come back frustrated after failing to get the same tool to complete trivial tasks.

To me, there are a couple of factors that may contribute to this that I haven't seen discussed all that much.

### #1. Ego is the enemy

Developers tend to have some degree emotional attachment to their work. I was very guilty of this in my junior years. I still vividly recall when, during a trip abroad, a colleague started filling in for me on a client's project, only for me to learn that while I was away, he had apparently rewritten most of the code I had spent weeks carefully writing.

In hindsight, it feels almost silly to think I was frustrated by that. Especially because my colleague was fully right. What I wrote originally was not a brilliancy, but more of a weird frankenstein that was a result of me being alone with the codebase for too long, with no one to review and discuss ideas with.

I like to think I improved a lot in that front. I still take get that big dopamine hit when I solve a complex problem, or design an elegant architecture. But I've also learned to accept that it can be fully scraped by next week. All it takes is a harmless change in requirements for a brilliant architecture to turn into a horrible one.

Where am I going with this, you ask? I think that success with AI tools is probably highly correlated with one's ability to not get too attached to our code, or to *our idea* of what the code should look like.
You need a certain level of detachment. Usually the code is just a means to an end.

Most developers who take pride in their work will probably look at an LLM's output with a very critical eye (and rightfully so). They'll find problems, areas to disagree, or simply places where they could do better themselves, whatever "better" means. The same has always happened 

### #2. "Quality"

Back in my junior days, we didn't have real-time linters or LSPs. Instead, we argued for days on Github about single versus double quotes, or whether a function was too long or unreadable according to some arbitrary metrics a book once taught us. Then we finally merged the damn thing, only to never have to look at it again because the product was scraped. Those were the days!

This is not to say that all code quality discussions are pointless. But it does hint that maybe there are other things we should care less about, and try to avoid pure perfectionism.

The industry already made a large jump forward when compilers and linters became more advanced (Typescript and Rust both excel at this) and integrated into CI pipelines. We all collectively stopped having to manually look out for lints that are now fully automated.

And now, with AI tools, we can one layer further, and potentially detach ourselves from portions of our code in the name of speed.
The extreme end of this is what the internet calls "vibe coding" (more on this later). But there is a middle ground, where you're able to still responsibly offload a lot of, while keeping any potential damage or code smells in check.

I like to think about code quality in terms of **ossification**.
When something ossifies (i.e., hardens and becomes central to the entire system, just like your bones) it becomes much more resistant to change. This resistance comes in many forms:

- A database schema bug that is hard to fix without bricking production data
- A bug in a function that has become expected behavior for many of its callers in the codebase
- Code relying on global state or side effects that were originally unrelated, but ended up tangled as the codebase grew
- A cryptographic vulnerability that is trivial for you to fix with a dependency update, except for the fact it invalidates all live user sessions the moment it goes live, causing a DDoS in your auth flow.
- Your tech stack choice, where a migration in frameworks or even programming languages would force a large rewrite
- A dependency on a 3rd party API that could shut down or change pricing a few years from now

## What is vibe-coding anyway?

I often browser [r/rust][reddit-rust], [r/webdev][reddit-webdev], and other similar communities to hear about new tooling. The most common trend there in past months is the huge increase in new projects being showcased, with varying levels of AI-assistance being visible, and being immediatelly dismised as vibe-coded slop.

While I largely agree with a lot of those assessments (there has indeed been a concerning amount of one-weekend projects being presented as if they were fully fledged OSS products), I find it frustrating that the label "vibe coding" ends up thrown around to all of them with no real distinction.

The term has become the main source of polarization. vibe-coded things are bad. everyone just takes a quick glance, counts up the amount of emojis in the readme, and determines their stance on the repo.

As usual with internet stuff, nuance goes down the drain. I too did what the kids call "vibe coding" on a few personal repos, with success rates all over the spectrum.

To me, vibe coding is when you not only don't care about the code itself, but you also don't care enough about the final result's quality, security, or edge cases. Or you don't know have enough technical background to be able to care in the first place.

Because, as I said in [Ego is the enemy][...], not caring about the code should already be the baseline. This is not to say that code quality and good engineering principles don't matter, though. They absolutely do, and will still mark the difference between an apparently good product and an *actually* good product. Sometimes that only surfaces late in the process (maybe in the form of a security vulnerability a vide-coder failed to notice)
